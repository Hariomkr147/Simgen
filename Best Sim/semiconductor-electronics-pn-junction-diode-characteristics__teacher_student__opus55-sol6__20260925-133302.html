Governing equations
I = Is[exp(VD/(ηVT)) − 1],   Vs = IR + VD
VT = kT/q ≈ 0.0259 V at 300 K;   Is(T) = 10−12 × 2(T − 300)/10 A
w = w0√[max(V0 − VD, 0.0025V0)/V0]
rd = ΔV/ΔI = ηVT/(I + Is)
Here V0 = 0.7 V and w0 = 1 µm. The width formula has a minimum-width cutoff.

What to observe

  • Forward current stays small until VD is roughly 0.6–0.7 V, then rises sharply.
  • Forward bias narrows the depletion layer; reverse bias widens it.
  • Reverse current approaches −Is and is nearly independent of further reverse voltage.
  • Increasing T raises Is and shifts the forward curve toward lower voltage.
  • As forward current increases, dynamic resistance rd falls.

Diffusion initially leaves fixed ions on either side of the junction, creating the depletion layer and barrier potential. Reverse current comes from the drift of thermally generated minority carriers. This idealized model does not include reverse breakdown.

Check your understanding

1. In reverse bias, what happens to the depletion layer width?

Decreases · Increases · Unchanged · Becomes zero
Increases. The applied voltage adds to the barrier, so more ions are uncovered and w grows.

2. Why is reverse current nearly constant?

Limited by minority carriers · Limited by R · Barrier vanishes · Majority carriers diffuse
Limited by minority carriers. Reverse current is drift of thermally generated minority carriers, which have a fixed supply at a given T.

3. At I = 10 mA and 300 K with η = 1, rd is about:

0.26 Ω · 2.6 Ω · 26 Ω · 260 Ω
2.6 Ω. rd = ηVT/I = 0.0259/0.010 ≈ 2.6 Ω.
